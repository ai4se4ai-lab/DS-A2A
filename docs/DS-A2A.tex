\title{\textsc{AgentM2M}: M2M Transformations for Agentic Collaborations in Software Engineering}

% --- Double-anonymous review: no author block in the submission. ---
% \author{...}

\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
Teams of LLM agents often fail at the seams: hand-offs are free text,
nothing records which artifacts correspond, and team structure is fixed at
design time. We propose \tool{}, which treats every
hand-off as an ATL-style model-to-model (M2M) transformation. Each agent
owns a view model conforming to its own metamodel. A deterministic engine
executes the \emph{structure} of a hand-off (matching, element creation,
reference resolution, trace links); the LLM only fills attribute values
through validated \emph{stochastic bindings}, each reading a bounded source
footprint. The LLM is called only for bindings whose footprint changed, so
the cost of a change is bounded by its obligations, not by model size.
Persisted trace models drive change propagation, and evolving the team at
runtime is a higher-order transformation. The design targets 11 of the 14
MAST failure modes (5 directly, 6 partially). In a pilot on 8 DevBench
repositories with two open-weight models, change impact was exact
(precision and recall 1.00) where baselines over-regenerated, propagating a
change cost 19--38$\times$ fewer tokens than re-running free-text
hand-offs, all 40 injected canary criteria reached the code (7 and 25 of 40
with free text), and a new agent joined at runtime with 7 lines of glue.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

LLM-based agents increasingly work in \emph{teams} that jointly analyze,
design, implement, and test software~\cite{he2025lma}, organized by
roles~\cite{hong2024metagpt,qian2024chatdev,tao2024magis} or by wired
conversation topologies~\cite{wu2024autogen,li2023camel,chen2024agentverse}.
The record is sobering. Cemri et al.\ annotated more than 1{,}600
traces of seven multi-agent frameworks and derived MAST, a taxonomy of 14
failure modes~\cite{cemri2025mast}. Gains over single agents were often
minimal. Of the failure-mode occurrences they report, 44.2\% are
\emph{system design issues}, 32.3\% \emph{inter-agent misalignment}, and
23.5\% \emph{task verification}; in their case studies, changing only the
team's \emph{structure} raised task success substantially.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Problem analysis.} We mapped each MAST mode to
the coordination deficit it manifests (Table~\ref{tab:mast}); the mapping
is analytical, built from the published definitions, and is in our artifact
(Section~\ref{sec:future}). Three deficits emerge; three further modes
(FM-1.1, 2.2, 2.6) are rooted in a single agent's reasoning, beyond the
reach of any coordination layer.

\noindent\textbf{P1: Lossy hand-offs between heterogeneous views.} Each
agent reasons over its own representation (stories, components, code,
tests), yet hand-offs are free text that another LLM re-interprets, so
information is silently dropped or invented (FM-1.4, 2.1, 2.4, 2.5).

\noindent\textbf{P2: No consistency or traceability under change.} Nothing
records which artifacts \emph{correspond}. Agents act on stale artifacts,
repeat work, drift, and verify the wrong thing (FM-1.3, 2.3, 3.1--3.3).

\noindent\textbf{P3: Collaboration structure lives in prompts.} Roles,
topology, and termination are stated in prompts. They are neither enforced
(FM-1.2, 1.5) nor changeable at runtime without rewriting the glue by hand.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Existing remedies.} MCP and A2A~\cite{mcp2024,a2a2025}
standardize \emph{how} messages travel, not \emph{what} they mean. Structured documents~\cite{hong2024metagpt} and
schema-validated shared state~\cite{zhang2026patchboard} make outputs
well-formed, but a single schema removes heterogeneity by fiat. Automated
agent-system design~\cite{zhuge2024gptswarm,hu2025adas,zhang2025aflow}
searches topologies offline, with no guarantee about what flows along an
edge.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Key insight.} Heterogeneous views, explicit
relations between them, consistency under change, and runtime evolution are
long-standing concerns of model-driven engineering (MDE): view-based
development~\cite{klare2021vitruvius,bruneliere2019views}, bidirectional
transformations (bx)~\cite{czarnecki2009bx,stevens2010qvt} and their
networks~\cite{stevens2020networks,klare2023termination},
models@run.time~\cite{blair2009runtime}, and higher-order transformations
(HOTs)~\cite{tisi2009hot}. MDE does \emph{not} cover what sets agent teams
apart: stochastic LLMs perform the transformations, and the team changes
while it runs.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Our approach.} \tool{} treats every hand-off as
an ATL-style M2M transformation~\cite{jouault2008atl} and splits each rule
along a line that ATL's execution model already draws. Matching, element
creation, and reference resolution stay with a deterministic engine. Only
the \emph{value} of a text or code attribute goes to an LLM, as a validated
\emph{stochastic binding} over a declared source footprint. Trace links,
transient in ATL~\cite{jouault2005traceability}, are persisted as trace
models~\cite{vara2014metagem}; changing the team is a HOT over a runtime
team model.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} \textbf{(C1)} A formulation of
agent teams as networks of hybrid deterministic/stochastic M2M
transformations (Section~\ref{sec:approach}). \textbf{(C2)} An open-source
prototype runtime with pluggable LLM backends, released with the
\devteam{} metamodels, rules, and MAST mapping. \textbf{(C3)} A pilot
evaluation on DevBench against free-text and shared-schema baselines
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Background and Running Example}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Model-to-model transformation with ATL}

An M2M transformation $T:\Mods{\MM_A}\to\Mods{\MM_B}$ maps models between
metamodels~\cite{jouault2008atl}. An ATL matched rule
\code{from s:A!C (g) to t:B!D (f <- e)} maps every element of type \cls{C}
satisfying OCL guard \code{g} to an element of type \cls{D} whose feature
\code{f} is set by the \emph{binding} \code{f <- e}. \emph{Matching}
allocates the targets of every match and records a \emph{trace link};
\emph{initialization} then evaluates bindings, \emph{resolving} each source
element a binding yields to the target generated from it. Sources are
read-only, targets write-only, and each source element is matched by at
most one rule, so the result is independent of rule order.

\subsection{Running example: \devteam{}}

\devteam{} is a four-agent team that builds a small to-do web service. The
\emph{Analyst} turns user requests into user stories grouped by epics, each
with acceptance criteria; the \emph{Architect} designs components and API
operations; the \emph{Developer} writes code edits; the \emph{Tester}
writes test cases with executable oracles and reports verdicts. The initial
requirements contain epic \code{E1} (\emph{task management}) with three
stories: \code{S1} \emph{create a task} (accepted; criterion \code{S1.1}: a
title is required), \code{S2} \emph{mark a task done} (accepted;
\code{S2.1}: completing a done task is rejected), and \code{S3}
\emph{export tasks as CSV} (still a draft; \code{S3.1}: the file has a
header row).

With free-text hand-offs, each deficit surfaces in this small team.
\emph{P1:} the Analyst's summary drops \code{S2.1}, so no operation handles
double completion and no test checks it (FM-2.4, surfacing as FM-3.2).
\emph{P2:} the Analyst later tightens \code{S2.1} to ``return HTTP 409''.
Nothing says which signature, code edit, or test depends on it, so the team
either regenerates everything or keeps stale artifacts (FM-1.3). \emph{P3:}
midway, the lead asks for a security review of every API operation, which
means rewiring prompts by hand. We follow these three episodes
through Section~\ref{sec:approach}.

\tool{} is used in seven steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). Steps~1--3 happen
at design time. Steps~4--7 are executed by the runtime in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:runtime}, whose circled numbers refer to the steps.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step~1: Give every agent its own form (view
metamodel).} Every artifact an agent $a_i$ produces is a model
$M_i\in\Mods{\MM_i}$ conforming to a view metamodel $\MM_i$ owned by
$a_i$, which fixes what it can state (Listing~\ref{lst:views}).

\begin{lstlisting}[language=KM3, float=t,
  caption={View metamodels of the \devteam{} agents.},
  label={lst:views}]
package Req {                            -- Analyst
 enumeration Status { literal draft; literal accepted; }
 class Epic      { attribute name : String; }
 class UserStory { attribute id, title : String;
   attribute status : Status;  reference epic : Epic;
   reference criteria[1-*] container : Criterion; }
 class Criterion { attribute id, text : String; } }
package Arch {                           -- Architect
 class Component { attribute name : String; }
 class Operation { attribute name, signature : String;
   reference component : Component; } }
-- Code: CodeEdit(file,body); Test: TestCase(name,oracle), TestRun(name,verdict)
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent An \cls{Operation} knows nothing about user stories. The views
stay heterogeneous; how they \emph{correspond} is recorded in trace models
(Step~6), not baked into a shared schema.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step~2: Describe the team as a model.} A team is
a megamodel $\team=(A,V,\mathbf{T},\omega,\varphi)$ of agents $A$, views
$V=\{\MM_i\}$, hand-off transformations $\mathbf{T}$, write rights
$\omega:A\to 2^{V}$, and an acceptance predicate $\varphi$ (Step~7). In
\devteam{}, $A=\{\mathit{an},\mathit{ar},\mathit{dv},\mathit{ts}\}$,
$\omega(\mathit{ar})=\{\MM_{\mathit{arch}}\}$ and likewise for the others,
and $\mathbf{T}$ holds $T_1$ (\code{Req2Arch}), $T_2$ (\code{Arch2Code}),
$T_3$ (\code{Code2Test}, one \cls{TestRun} per code edit), and $T_4$
(\code{Criterion2Test}); hand-offs into one view create disjoint element
types, so no target has two producers. $T_2$ is multi-source: the Developer reads both
$M_{\mathit{arch}}$ and $M_{\mathit{req}}$. Through $\omega$, ATL's
discipline carries over: an agent reads upstream views and writes only its
own.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step~3: Write each hand-off as hybrid rules.} A
hand-off $T_{ij}:\Mods{\MM_i}\to\Mods{\MM_j}$ is a set of ATL-style rules
$r=(p_r,g_r,t_r,B_r)$: a source pattern $p_r$ with OCL guard $g_r$, a
target pattern $t_r$, and bindings $B_r$. A hand-off may read several views
(as $T_2$ does); its matches then range over their disjoint union.

\begin{definition}[Hybrid hand-off]\label{def:hybrid}
$T=(R^{\str},B^{\sem},\mathcal{L},\mathit{chk})$: the structural part
$R^{\str}$ (patterns, guards, target patterns, OCL bindings), stochastic
bindings $B^{\sem}$, the target owner's LLM $\mathcal{L}$ with a
distribution $\Dist{\pi}$ over values per prompt $\pi$, and a validator
$\mathit{chk}_b$ per $b\in B^{\sem}$.
\end{definition}

\noindent Thus $B_r=B_r^{\str}\uplus B_r^{\sem}$. A stochastic binding $b\in B_r^{\sem}$ has the form
\code{f <- @llm(prompt, e)}. Its OCL expression $e$ defines the
\emph{footprint} $\fp_b(m)$ of a match $m$: the source locations (object,
feature) that evaluating $e$ reads, whose values are the only source data
the LLM may see. \code{@check} names the validator $\mathit{chk}_b$ (a
type/OCL constraint, a parser, or an executable oracle). Stochastic
bindings target primitive-typed attributes, never references, and the LLM
is reserved for values no OCL expression can compute
(Listing~\ref{lst:rules}).

\begin{lstlisting}[language=ATL, float=t,
  caption={Hybrid hand-offs $T_1$ and $T_4$ (\code{@llm}, \code{@check}).},
  label={lst:rules}]
module Req2Arch; create OUT : Arch from IN : Req; -- T1
rule Epic2Component { from e : Req!Epic
 to c : Arch!Component ( name <- e.name ) }
rule Story2Operation {
 from s : Req!UserStory
          (s.status = #accepted)        -- guard
 to  op : Arch!Operation (
   name      <- s.title.toOpName(),     -- structural
   component <- s.epic,          -- resolved via trace
   signature <- @llm('Derive an API signature',
          Sequence{s.title, s.criteria}), -- footprint
   @check signature.parses()
          and signature.params->notEmpty() ) }
module Criterion2Test; create OUT:Test from IN:Req; -- T4
rule Criterion2TestCase {
 from c : Req!Criterion
   (c.refImmediateComposite().status = #accepted)
 to  tc : Test!TestCase ( name <- c.id,
   oracle <- @llm('Write a test oracle', c.text),
   @check oracle.compiles() and oracle.failsOn(stub) ) }
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent In \code{Story2Operation}, whether $\mathit{op}_{S2}$ exists,
what it is called, and which component owns it are all deterministic. The
LLM writes only its signature, and it sees nothing but \code{S2}'s title
and criteria.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\tool{} runtime (\step{n}: Step~n).}
\label{alg:runtime}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require team $\team$, models $M_i$, traces $\TL_{ij}$, budget $k$
\State $E\gets\emptyset$;\ $D\gets\mathbf{T}$
  \Comment{escalations; hand-offs to run}
\Loop
  \While{$D\neq\emptyset$} \Comment{\step{4}\,\step{6}}
    \State take $T_{ij}$ from $D$; run $R^{\str}$; update $\TL_{ij}$
      \label{alg:struct}
    \ForAll{stochastic binding $(t,b)$ with a stale stamp}
      \label{alg:obl}
      \If{$\#\fp_b$ equals the failed stamp of $(t,b)$}
        \State $E\gets E\cup\{(t,b)\}$ \label{alg:memo}
          \Comment{no LLM call}
      \Else
        \State \textsc{Bind}: sample $\le k$ values from $\fp_b$ until
          $\mathit{chk}_b$, else $E\gets E\cup\{(t,b)\}$, failed stamp
          $\gets\#\fp_b$ \label{alg:bind}
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State \textbf{if} $M_j$ changed \textbf{then} $D\gets D\cup\mathit{readers}(M_j)$
  \EndWhile
  \State \textbf{if} $\varphi$ \textbf{then return} done;
    \textbf{if} $E\neq\emptyset$ \textbf{then return} $E$
    \Comment{\step{7}}
  \State $\Delta\gets\textsc{Lift}(x,M_i)$ for text $x$ of agent $a$,
    $\MM_i\in\omega(a)$ \label{alg:lift} \Comment{\step{5}}
  \State \textbf{if} $\Delta=\bot$ \textbf{then} $E\gets E\cup\{x\}$
    \textbf{else} apply $\Delta$; $D\gets\mathit{readers}(M_i)$
\EndLoop
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step~4: Run a hand-off.}
Line~\ref{alg:struct} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:runtime} is ATL's two-phase
execution, made incremental~\cite{jouault2010incremental}: it creates or
deletes targets and trace links as matches appear or vanish and evaluates
structural bindings.
Lines~\ref{alg:obl}--\ref{alg:bind} re-bind every stochastic binding whose
\emph{version stamp} (a digest of its footprint at acceptance) is missing
or stale, prompting with the footprint only and escalating after $k$
rejections. Accepted bindings are then checked jointly against $\MM_j$'s
OCL constraints (e.g., all signatures agree on \code{TaskId}); a violation
escalates the bindings involved.

On $T_1$, \code{S3} fails the guard, and the engine creates
$\mathit{comp}_{E1}$, $\mathit{op}_{S1}$, $\mathit{op}_{S2}$ with three
trace links. For $\mathit{op}_{S2}$, a first sample \code{markTaskDone()}
fails the \code{params} check; \code{markTaskDone(id: TaskId): Task} is
accepted and stamped. On $T_4$, \code{S3.1} fails the guard, but
\code{S2.1} cannot be dropped: \code{Criterion2TestCase} matches it, so its
test case exists by construction: the P1 episode is closed by a rule, not a
prompt.

\emph{Token cost.} Free-text teams pay for repetition \emph{across roles},
as each role re-reads the upstream transcript. \tool{} pays per binding:
for the set $S$ of bindings re-bound in a run,
$C=\sum_{(t,b)\in S}\sum_{i\le n_{t,b}}(P+|\pi_b|+|\fp_b|+|v_i|)$
(preamble, prompt, footprint, output). A binding is sampled only if its
footprint digest differs from its stamp and from its \emph{failed stamp}
(line~\ref{alg:memo}), so an escalated binding is re-reported, not
re-sampled. Validators judge the extracted value, not its wrapping, and
return a reason that the resample prompt carries. Prompts cap answer
length, shared context is held once outside all footprints, and a one-line
system prompt replaces verbose defaults. A run makes at most $k\cdot|S|$
calls, and the input per call does not grow with the conversation.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step~5: Turn free text into models.} Agents
still think in text. \textsc{Lift} (line~\ref{alg:lift}) is the only way
text enters the system: an LLM renders it as elements of the author's own
view, rejected unless $M_i\oplus\Delta$ conforms to $\MM_i$ and its OCL
constraints; a story lacking its mandatory \code{epic} is resampled. Agents
thus add structure beyond the hand-off skeleton (the Architect adds a
\cls{Store} component). Hand-off targets stay engine-owned, so each
hand-off remains a function of its source models.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step~6: Handle change with trace models and
obligations.} \tool{} persists each trace as a trace
model~\cite{vara2014metagem}
$\TL_{ij}\subseteq\mathit{Match}(M_i)\times M_j\times R$ with footprints
and stamps. A change $\Delta$ on $M_i$ that modifies the locations
$\mathit{foot}(\Delta)$ re-runs $R^{\str}$ deterministically and yields the
\emph{obligations}
\begin{align*}
\Obl(\Delta)=\{(t,b)\mid{}&(m,t,r)\in \TL_{ij},\ b\in B_r^{\sem},\\
  &\fp_b(m)\cap \mathit{foot}(\Delta)\neq\emptyset\},
\end{align*}
the stale stamps of line~\ref{alg:obl}, each addressed to $\MM_j$'s owner. For a change, $S=\Obl(\Delta)$, so it costs at most
$k\cdot|\Obl(\Delta)|$ LLM calls however large the models are. Take the P2
episode. When the Analyst tightens \code{S2.1},
$\Obl=\{(\mathit{op}_{S2},\code{signature}),
(\mathit{tc}_{S2.1},\code{oracle}), (\mathit{ed}_{S2},\code{body})\}$.
$T_2$'s \code{body} binding reads $\mathit{op}.\code{signature}$ and
\code{S2}'s criteria, so the code edit is obligated directly, even if the
re-sampled signature does not change (a 409 need not alter it).
$\mathit{op}_{S1}$ and $\mathit{tc}_{S1.1}$ have disjoint footprints and
are left alone. Every re-accepted value is itself a change on $M_j$, so
obligations propagate hop by hop; an identical re-sample cuts only the
paths that depend on it alone. For cyclic networks~\cite{stevens2020networks},
we schedule $D$ with Klare and Gleitze's conservative strategy, which
provably terminates but may fail~\cite{klare2023termination}; failures are
escalated.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Step~7: Know when the team is done.} The engine,
not an agent, decides $\varphi$: every match is covered by a trace link, no
obligation or escalation is open, all stamps are fresh, and all validators
pass, including the oracles the engine runs for each \cls{TestRun} of
$T_3$. A Tester declaring success early cannot end the run while
$\mathit{tc}_{S2.1}$'s new oracle fails (FM-1.5, 3.1): termination is a
property of the models, not a claim in a conversation.

\subsection{Evolving the team with higher-order transformations}
\label{subsec:hot}

The team model $\team$ is kept causally connected to the running team
(models@run.time~\cite{blair2009runtime}). Like
MeTAGeM-Trace~\cite{vara2014metagem}, which turns relation models into
trace-generating ATL via HOTs, \tool{} derives hand-offs from relation
models, but at runtime and lifted (Step~5) from the lead's request. The P3
episode,
\emph{add a Security Reviewer with view $\MM_{\mathit{sec}}$ related to
\cls{Operation}}, becomes a relation model that a HOT~\cite{tisi2009hot}
turns into a trace-generating hand-off $T_5$, a new entry in $\omega$, and
a strengthened $\varphi$ (dashed in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), applied
between outer-loop iterations of Algorithm~\ref{alg:runtime}. As $T_5$'s
rules match existing models, the reviewer receives obligations for
$\mathit{op}_{S1}$ and $\mathit{op}_{S2}$ on arrival, with no hand-written
glue.

\section{Preliminary Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\noindent\textbf{Setup.} Our prototype (C2) instantiates \devteam{}'s views
and hand-offs with one simplification: $T_2$ is single-source, because
$T_1$ structurally copies each story's criteria into its operation. We compare it with \emph{free-text} hand-offs
and a PatchBoard-style \emph{shared schema}~\cite{zhang2026patchboard}
under identical roles and LLM. Subjects are the 8 Python repositories of
DevBench~\cite{li2024devbench} whose reference implementations pass their
own tests in our environment. Each operation's criterion carries its UML
signature hint; module-wide criteria are given once. We use two local
open-weight models, \texttt{qwen2.5-coder:7b} and \texttt{qwen3.8:27b}.
They differ in size, specialization, generation, and verbosity; if
\tool{}'s guarantees follow from its rules, they should hold on both.
Ollama reports exact per-call token counts. All configurations use
temperature 0.2, one seed, $k{=}2$, a 4096-token output cap, a one-line
system prompt, and thinking disabled. With eight repositories, one model family, and one seed, the pilot is a
feasibility signal, not a powered comparison.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Research questions.}
\textbf{RQ1 (fidelity):} Does \tool{} reduce P1 failure modes, and at what
build cost? Besides MAST-judged modes, we inject five \emph{canary}
criteria per repository, each pinned to an unguessable constant (the role
\code{S2.1} plays in P1), and count those reaching the code.
\textbf{RQ2 (change):} How precise, complete, and costly is change
propagation? We inject three PRD changes per repository (tighten, add, and
remove a criterion), the first mirroring the P2 episode. Baselines, lacking an impact set, re-run on the changed PRD.
\textbf{RQ3 (evolution):} Can a HOT alone add an agent mid-run, as in the
P3 episode?

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Results} (Table~\ref{tab:results}).
\emph{RQ1:} On both models, all 40 canary constants reached \tool{}'s code.
Free text kept 7 (7B) and 25 (27B), shared schema 0 and 5. Canaries show that
stated constants survive a hand-off, not that the code is correct: no
configuration passed DevBench's acceptance tests, because per-operation
requirements carry the signature, not the PRD's detailed behavior. The MAST
judge, an LLM proxy for an unreleased annotator, flagged few modes and may
be miscalibrated for per-binding transcripts (\tool{}'s P2 count). Building cost \tool{} 11.7k and 12.5k tokens, as
prompts bound each answer, while free text doubled (8.3k to 16.5k). \tool{} was
cheaper to build than free text on 2 of 8 repositories with the 7B model
and on 7 of 8 with the 27B model. Shared schema, with 3 calls and no
executable oracles, was cheapest. The Step~4 cost controls matter: an
earlier prototype without them cost 2--3.4$\times$ free text to build.

\emph{RQ2} gave the clearest result. \tool{} reached precision and recall
1.00 on all 48 changes, as the obligation operator predicts. Shared schema
had recall 1.00 but precision 0.14, since it regenerates everything; free
text both missed and over-reported impacts (0.65--0.79). A change cost
\tool{} 0.42--0.45k tokens (0 for a removal, which is purely structural),
19--38$\times$ less than free text and 9--17$\times$ less than shared
schema. With the 7B model, \tool{}'s extra build cost over free text is
recovered by the first change, and the advantage grows with every change a
team absorbs.

\emph{RQ3:} On both models, the reviewer added by a HOT covered all 118
existing operations, with 7 lines of glue against 11 per baseline; like
RQ2's exactness, this follows from the rules, not the model. One limit holds throughout: \textsc{Lift}
remains a free-text boundary, and content it omits is invisible to every
check.

\section{Future Plans}\label{sec:future}

Three research challenges (RC) remain. \RC{1}~\emph{Learning the glue:}
an architect agent will propose metamodels and relation models, admitted
only after conformance and model checks. \RC{2}~\emph{Bidirectionality:}
when a developer finds a criterion infeasible, intent must flow back; we
will lift hand-offs to bx and characterize
correctness and hippocraticness~\cite{stevens2010qvt} when the backward
direction is stochastic. \RC{3}~\emph{Evidence at scale:} all Python
DevBench repositories, paired tests, more model families, and MetaGPT- and ChatDev-style
teams~\cite{hong2024metagpt,qian2024chatdev} ported to \tool{}; failing to
reduce P1 modes against a shared schema would refute our central claim.
Two independent coders will re-code the MAST mapping (Cohen's $\kappa$).
